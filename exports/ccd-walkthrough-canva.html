CLA Report Card 2026 · England · 2024/25

Since EBacc, arts in English state schools have been squeezed.

Since the English Baccalaureate was introduced in 2010, expressive arts in English state schools have been squeezed on time, entries, teachers and access. The Cultural Learning Alliance Report Card 2026 covers the 2024/25 academic year.

Time, entries, teachers, access — the pressure is structural, not seasonal.

2010
EBacc introduced — the policy turn that reshaped the arts in English state schools.

Why now · the long drop · CLA 2026

Expressive arts GCSE entries are down 41.7% since 2009/10.

Expressive arts were 14% of GCSEs in 2009/10 and 6.8% now. Drama GCSE −40.6%, Dance −55.1%, Music −25.6% (Music +5.7% last year), D&T −71.3%. 36% of secondaries entered nobody for GCSE Music; the same for Drama; 83% nobody for Dance. Arts A-level −9.8% in one year (2023/24–2024/25) and −25% since 2011/12. Music A-level −44.7% since 2010/11, Drama A-level −49.5%, Dance A-level −50.5% since 2014/15. KS4 EBacc hours +30% since 2010; expressive arts hours −26%; KS5 arts hours −35%.

When entries fall this far, the pipeline into HE and the creative industries thins with them.

Why now · primary · CLA / Teacher Tapp

Primary arts time is unequal — and thinning.

47% of independent primary teachers report more than 2.5 hours of arts a week versus 6% in the state sector. Around 1 in 4 primary teachers report less than one hour. 28% say hours have fallen in the last two years (31% in the most FSM-heavy schools). 43% have no external cultural partner. Only 9% of primaries have a Drama lead; 5% a Dance lead. Hours figures from CLA / Teacher Tapp (about 2,900 teachers).

Non-specialists carry the load — with little time, and little external partnership.

Why now · workforce & HE · CLA 2026

The teacher pipeline is 6,000 below 2010/11.

Vacancies in Art & Design and Music are 3.8× 2010 levels. Music ITT −51.3% since 2010/11; D&T −52.3%. Government met 40% of the Music ITT target in 2024/25 and 49% for Drama (was 79% the prior year). Just under 23% of expressive arts teachers have no subject-relevant qualification beyond A-level; Music +10% in one year. £10k Music and Art & Design ITT bursaries were scrapped. HE Creative Arts & Design applications −3% in 2025 vs 2024, −7.6% vs the 2022 peak. 9 of 10 universities with the steepest falls in home Creative Arts undergraduates are modern universities. Roehampton, Kent and Cumbria each lost two-thirds of Creative Arts academic staff since 2014/15.

When bursaries go and ITT targets are missed, classrooms feel it first.

Why now · entitlement · CLA 2026 / EPI

Arts entitlement tracks deprivation.

54% of schools in the most deprived fifth of local authorities have no GCSE Music entries versus 21% in the least deprived; for Drama it is 59% versus 17%. FSM pupils are under-represented in every arts GCSE. Music A-level: 3.8% FSM versus 9.3% of all A-level students. Music has the largest GCSE disadvantage gap: poorer pupils are 20.1 months behind and 38% less likely to take it (EPI 2020, cited by CLA). Black and Asian students are under-represented in every arts A-level.

Where need is greatest, access is thinnest.

The problem

Arts teachers are working alone.

Drama, dance and music teachers stitch a year together from PDFs, shared drives, photocopied schemes and last year's tabs — often as the only specialist in the building. Great practice gets lost; non-specialists are left guessing.

5+
hours a week the average teacher spends planning outside of class — most of it reinventing what someone, somewhere has already made.
01

Practice lives in too many places

Schemes in folders, rehearsal notes on a phone, warm-ups in a notebook. Nothing connects, nothing carries forward.

02

The bigger arc is invisible

It's hard to see how a term, a key stage, or a whole creative journey fits together — let alone shape it without starting over.

03

Creative practice gets lost

Teaching is creative work — but the warm-up that worked, the rehearsal that landed, the unit a colleague nailed disappear into forgotten documents by July, learned again the hard way next September.

Why now · the case for the arts

The arts in higher education are at a tipping point.

Drama, music and dance courses at UK universities are being merged, paused and closed. The pipeline that feeds the West End, our orchestras, our touring dance companies — and every primary classroom that sings — starts in the schools we teach in today. If the practice we share now is thin, the cohort that arrives at university in five years will be too.

"We don't get a second chance at a generation of artists."
